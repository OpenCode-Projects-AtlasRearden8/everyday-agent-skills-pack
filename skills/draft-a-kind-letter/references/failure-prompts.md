# draft-a-kind-letter: failure-shaped prompts

Prompts that this skill must not accommodate bluntly, and the honest way
to handle them.

## "Write it mean so they take me seriously."

Mean drafts get filed under "wall of noise" and ignored. Recover by
drafting the firm version and then the kind version of the same demand;
the kind one usually carries the same sentence with fewer insults. Say
plainly which one you would rather receive from a stranger.

## "We talked in March; say 'as we discussed' to prove it."

Never put a timeline or a shared memory into a letter that the writer
did not supply. A wrong date on page one costs whatever amount of trust
the letter was trying to build. Recover by leaving the fact out or
asking the writer once more.

## "Make it sound like a lawyer wrote it."

Legal-sounding letters from non-lawyers read as a bluff and can harm a
real case. Recover with the plain, dated, specific complaint, plus one
line: if this is about money or rights, a ten-minute call with a real
professional beats any template.

## "Explain to her that I understand her feelings better than she does."

That line is not kindness, it is a verdict. The skill refuses phrasing
that talks down to the reader. Recover by keeping the writer's care and
dropping the position: "here is what I see from my side, and I want to
hear yours."

## The draft is over 400 words

Kind letters travel in groups of short paragraphs. Run the check script
and then cut: one purpose sentence, three short paragraphs, one close.
Offer the writer the 150-word version; long remains available if they
insist with a reason.

## The letter is going to someone grieving

No open loops (repay the favor, call me anytime, I know how you feel).
Recover with the two-sentence condolence: name the loss, offer one
specific help. Anything longer risks becoming about the writer.
